Whether any published diagnostic accuracy study exists for each of these pediatric foot findings is the deciding factor, since most of these conditions are defined clinically and may only have case series. Let me check the accuracy data for subtalar stiffness in tarsal coalition, the calcaneal squeeze test in Sever disease, and site-specific pain or tenderness in accessory navicular and Iselin disease.  
  
  
  
**Only one of the six codes (PIED-P35) has a published accuracy study, and one more (PIED-P31) has indirect data from which accuracy can be calculated; the other four have no data.** No meta-analysis of diagnostic accuracy and no primary-care or emergency cohort turned up for any of the four diagnoses.

**How the provisional values fare**

**PIED-P30 (LR 5): unsupported.** The only figure found is from an uncontrolled adult foot-and-ankle clinic series, in which subtalar motion was decreased in 23 of 32 feet (72%) with coalition.[<u>\[3\]</u>](https://doi.org/10.1177/107110070002100807) Without a comparison group, specificity and LRs cannot be derived. Rigid flatfoot with minimal subtalar motion also occurs without coalition, as shown in idiopathic rigid and peroneal spastic flatfoot series.<u>[\[4\]](https://doi.org/10.1177/107110070002100111)[\[5\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36905626)</u> The finding is also hard to reproduce: a study of 11 orthopedic surgeons found agreement coefficients of 0.07–0.11 for clinical subtalar measurement.[<u>\[6\]</u>](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9127113)  
  
**PIED-P31 (LR+ 6, LR− 0.3): only indirect data, which do not fit the intended use.** In the Kamel study every participant already had an accessory navicular, and the comparison is edematous versus non-edematous ossicles.[<u>\[1\]</u>](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32556471) The specificity therefore says nothing about separating accessory navicular from other causes of medial midfoot pain. Navicular bone stress injury, for example, produces navicular tenderness in 96% of adolescent cases.[<u>\[7\]</u>](https://doi.org/10.1177/03635465231170399)  
  
**PIED-P32 (LR+ 6, LR− 0.3): no data.** One specialist orthopedic hospital study recorded navicular tuberosity tenderness in 110 patients, but it tested MRI edema against tenderness (the reverse direction), and the abstract gives no counts.[<u>\[8\]</u>](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30376958) The full text may contain a 2×2 table that could be inverted.  
  
**PIED-P33 (LR+ 5, LR− 0.4): no data.** The Iselin literature consists of case reports, small series, and a descriptive systematic review.<u>[\[9\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28842092)[\[10\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1732301)</u> A valid study would be difficult to design because the apophysis is a normal radiographic finding and the diagnosis rests on history and examination.<u>[\[11\]](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD015156.pub2/full?utm_source=openevidence&utm_medium=referral)[\[12\]](https://doi.org/10.3113/fai.2011.0526)</u>  
  
**PIED-P34 (LR+ 5, LR− 0.3): no data.** No study tested heel pain location as an index test for Sever disease.[<u>\[2\]</u>](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24072363) A pilot study validated a self-report screening questionnaire for calcaneal apophysitis against same-day clinical examination in 63 children, with an AUC of 0.83, but the abstract gives no item-level accuracy.[<u>\[13\]</u>](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39314841)  
  
**PIED-P35 (LR+ 5, LR− 0.3): published point estimates are much stronger, but almost certainly inflated.** The lower confidence bound for the corrected LR+ is about 2, so the provisional value of 5 lies within the interval.[<u>\[2\]</u>](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24072363)

**Limits of the Perhamre study**

\- **Healthy controls.** Specificity was measured against pain-free children, not children with other causes of heel pain. Calcaneal stress injury, for example, also gives a positive squeeze test.<u>[\[2\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24072363)[\[14\]](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12178-026-10063-6)</u>  
  
- **Probable incorporation bias.** The same group's 2012 trial defined Sever injury by posterior calcaneal tenderness plus a positive squeeze test, so the reference standard likely includes the index test. This needs confirmation in the full text.[<u>\[15\]</u>](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0838.2010.01266.x?utm_source=openevidence&utm_medium=referral)  
  
- **Secondary citations are not independent.** American Family Physician reviews from 2023 and 2025 repeat the same sensitivity and specificity. The 2025 table footnote attributes them to a surgical reference standard, which does not match the Perhamre design.<u>[\[16\]](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2023/1200/sports-related-overuse-injuries)[\[17\]](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2025/1200/heel-pain)[\[2\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24072363)</u>  
  
- **Counts are reconstructed.** The 29/30 figure is inferred from the reported 97% and should be checked against the full text, including whether children or heels were counted.[<u>\[2\]</u>](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24072363)  
  
These results come from a literature database search, not a formal systematic review, so an unindexed study could have been missed.

Most of these are bedside findings whose accuracy has rarely been measured against a reference standard, so the key question for each code is whether a diagnostic accuracy study exists at all. Let me check the published accuracy data for each diagnosis group.  
  
  
  
**None of the 25 current likelihood ratios is confirmed by a published diagnostic accuracy study.** Only two codes (P8, P14) have sensitivity and specificity for the finding itself, and both give weaker LRs than the current values. One further code (P16) has a pooled sensitivity-type figure without specificity, and the remaining 22 have no data. No meta-analysis of history or examination accuracy was found for any of these diagnoses. No confidence intervals were found for any LR in the table. LRs marked "calc." are arithmetic from the reported sensitivity and specificity, not estimates.

**Corrections and structural issues**

\- **P8:** The only accuracy data are for patient self-assessment against a clinician's grading. No study of clinician inspection against the radiographic hallux valgus angle reported sensitivity or specificity; the Manchester scale was validated by correlation only.<u>[\[4\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20854678)[\[31\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15901901)</u>  
  
- **P14:** The current LR− of 1 is not consistent with the one available dataset, where absence of self-reported gout gave an LR− of 0.28. That cohort came from rheumatology clinics, so the value may not transfer to first presentations in primary care.[<u>\[9\]</u>](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/acr.22896?utm_source=openevidence&utm_medium=referral)  
  
- **P15:** A positive LR for gout has no published support. Podagra is rare in acute CPP crystal arthritis, and CPPD is the condition most often mislabelled as gout.<u>[\[32\]](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1511117?utm_source=openevidence&utm_medium=referral)[\[10\]](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41584-018-0078-5)</u>  
  
- **P16 and P17:** These derive from monoarthritis cohorts, where the first MTP joint is compared with other joints. Redness was not independently predictive in the primary care cohort. No study measures how well either finding separates gout from other causes of great-toe pain.[<u>\[12\]</u>](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/10.1001/archinternmed.2010.196?utm_source=openevidence&utm_medium=referral)  
  
- **P22 and P23:** These code the same sign (plantar midfoot ecchymosis) with different LR− values. Scoring both would count one finding twice. The sign also occurs with calcaneal and Chopart injuries, and ecchymosis may appear only days after injury.<u>[\[33\]](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00402-023-05040-4)[\[34\]](https://doi.org/10.1177/1071100716675293)</u>  
  
- **P28:** An LR+ of 30 requires, by arithmetic, that no more than 3.3% of foot-pain patients without Charcot have diabetes or neuropathy, even at 100% sensitivity. No specificity data exist to support this.[<u>\[24\]</u>](https://doi.org/10.1002/dmrr.3653?utm_source=openevidence&utm_medium=referral)  
  
- **P5–P7:** Accuracy is hard to measure because plantar fasciitis has no accepted reference standard, and studies define cases by these same features.<u>[\[35\]](https://doi.org/10.1177/1071100718811631)[\[3\]](https://doi.org/10.1177/107110070302400309)</u>  
  
- **P18:** Morton neuroma studies enrol patients who are already clinically diagnosed or operated, so almost none are disease-negative and specificity cannot be estimated.<u>[\[26\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25432459)[\[36\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37353000)</u>

**Limits of this search**

Per-item figures for P12, P16 and P17 probably exist in full-text tables that were not retrieved (Zammit 2011, Janssens 2010, Taylor 2015).<u>[\[7\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21619934)[\[12\]](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/10.1001/archinternmed.2010.196?utm_source=openevidence&utm_medium=referral)[\[13\]](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/acr.22585?utm_source=openevidence&utm_medium=referral)</u> Three Morton neuroma papers (Owens 2011, Pastides 2012, Cloke 2006) could not be retrieved; one reportedly has a control group. PMIDs are given only where they were retrieved.

\- **PIED-P1 (LR+ 3, LR− 0.2): not supported.** By back-calculation, this pair implies sensitivity of about 86% and specificity of about 71%. The sensitivity matches the surgical-series figure, but no study measured specificity. The Cochrane review describes the link between neuroma symptoms and tight footwear as inconclusive.<u>[\[1\]](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/10.1001/jama.2023.23906?utm_source=openevidence&utm_medium=referral)[\[6\]](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD014687.pub2/full?utm_source=openevidence&utm_medium=referral)</u>  
  
- **PIED-P2 (LR+ 3, LR− 0.1): not supported.** The pair implies sensitivity of about 93% and specificity of about 69%. Sensitivity is close to the published 96%, but specificity has not been measured.[<u>\[2\]</u>](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25432459)  
  
- **PIED-P3 (LR+ 4, LR− 0.6): LR+ confirmed; LR− is closer to 0.5.** The current 0.6 sits at the upper confidence limit of the derived estimate.[<u>\[3\]</u>](https://doi.org/10.1177/1071100720946748)  
  
- **PIED-P4 (LR+ 4, LR− 0.3): not supported.** The pair implies sensitivity of about 76% and specificity of about 81%. Published data give sensitivity only, from series of confirmed fractures with no non-fractured comparison group.<u>[\[4\]](https://doi.org/10.1177/1071100715600495)[\[5\]](https://doi.org/10.1177/03635465231170399)</u>

**Caveats that affect use of these numbers**

**Mulder click.** The likelihood ratios and confidence intervals were calculated here from the published counts (true positives 80, false positives 6, false negatives 55, true negatives 34), not reported by the authors.[<u>\[3\]</u>](https://doi.org/10.1177/1071100720946748) The reference standard was the same maneuver observed under ultrasound, and the clinical test was performed together with the thumb-finger squeeze, so incorporation bias is likely.[<u>\[3\]</u>](https://doi.org/10.1177/1071100720946748) The counts should be checked against the full text before adoption. Reported sensitivity varies widely, up to 98% in a histology-confirmed surgical series.[<u>\[7\]</u>](https://doi.org/10.1177/1071100716674998)  
  
**Squeeze test.** All patients in the Mahadevan study were selected on clinical criteria, and ultrasound confirmed a neuroma in 53 of 54 feet.[<u>\[2\]</u>](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25432459) This design inflates sensitivity and makes specificity uninterpretable. The Cochrane review states that no single criterion, including this test, gives enough certainty to diagnose Morton neuroma.[<u>\[6\]</u>](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD014687.pub2/full?utm_source=openevidence&utm_medium=referral)  
  
**Composite clinical assessment.** Where specificity has been measured against histology, it was low and imprecise: 0.33 (95% CI 0.01–0.91) for the main clinical symptoms combined, with sensitivity 0.94 (0.86–0.98).[<u>\[8\]</u>](https://doi.org/10.1177/1071100714540888) This applies to the composite, not to any single coded item.  
  
**N-spot.** The 81% figure is attributed to Torg's 1982 series in one review, and other reviews repeat it without a clear primary source.<u>[\[4\]](https://doi.org/10.1177/1071100715600495)[\[9\]](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12178-017-9392-9)</u> It should be verified against the primary paper. A systematic review of clinical tests for lower-limb stress fractures included only therapeutic ultrasound and tuning fork studies, with no palpation study.[<u>\[10\]</u>](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22813530)  
  
**PMIDs.** Only the Mahadevan PMID was retrieved from an indexed record; the others need a PubMed check.[<u>\[2\]</u>](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25432459)  
  
Would it help to look at how other evidence-based questionnaires handle items with sensitivity-only data, such as flagging them as expert-estimated or omitting the likelihood ratio?

**Where published data conflict with the current estimates**

Sex ratios from incidence or prevalence data only approximate ratios within a consulting population, because women make up 54–55% of foot and ankle consulters.<u>[\[54\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31109927)[\[1\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20403912)</u>  
  
- **F2 hallux valgus:** the multipliers imply about 6:1 female to male; published ratios are about 2.3–3:1. GP management peaks at 45–64 years rather than rising to age 80.<u>[\[6\]](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/acr.24075?utm_source=openevidence&utm_medium=referral)[\[8\]](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00296-017-3817-z)</u>  
  
- **F19 Freiberg:** the multipliers imply about 8.6:1; the literature gives about 5:1, and 68% female in adolescents.<u>[\[44\]](https://doi.org/10.3113/fai-2009-0167)[\[45\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42474446)</u>  
  
- **F9 Morton neuroma:** the multipliers imply about 2.5:1; primary care data give 1.74:1.[<u>\[23\]</u>](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16421136)  
  
- **F3 lesser toe deformity:** the multipliers imply about 2.1:1; prevalence ratios are 1:1 at 51–60 years and about 1.5:1 at 71–80 years.[<u>\[9\]</u>](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/acr.22040?utm_source=openevidence&utm_medium=referral)  
  
- **F20 Iselin relative to F21 Sever:** the estimates put Iselin at 25–40% of Sever's frequency at ages 11–13. In the Danish cohort, all "other apophysitis" totalled 13 cases against 601 Sever cases.[<u>\[51\]</u>](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02328-w)  
  
- **F17 tarsal coalition relative to F21 Sever:** the estimates put coalition at one eighth of Sever at age 12. Population incidence is about 8 per 100,000 per year at 10–14 years for coalition, against 3.7 per 1,000 for Sever at 6–17 years. These come from different populations, so this is an order-of-magnitude check only.<u>[\[41\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33186000)[\[50\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24297670)</u>  
  
- **F14 turf toe:** 0.8% at age 20 is far below its share of collegiate athletic foot injuries, but that denominator is trainer-recorded injuries, not clinic foot pain.[<u>\[37\]</u>](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12178-023-09870-y)  
  
Estimates broadly compatible with available data: F1 adult values sit at or slightly above the 11–15% and 12.1% figures; F7 male:female 4:1 is at the upper end of the published range; F18 and F21 sex ratios fall within published ranges; the F8 value at 12 years matches the paediatric fracture share.<u>[\[3\]](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMcp032745?utm_source=openevidence&utm_medium=referral)[\[2\]](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-019-2718-6)[\[18\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33798500)[\[42\]](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11759391/)[\[53\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37713414)[\[19\]](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00431-024-05590-8)</u>  
  
The Sever age anchors (peak at 9–11, steep fall after 13) agree with the Danish cohort, where mean age at diagnosis was 10.8 ± 1.4 years.[<u>\[51\]</u>](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02328-w)

**Limits of the evidence**

\- In UK general practice, 28% of foot and ankle consultations carry only the non-specific code "foot pain", so coded data under-count every specific diagnosis.[<u>\[1\]</u>](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20403912)  
  
- Only 9.1% of people aged 50 and over with foot problems consulted their GP within 18 months, so population prevalence cannot be converted to consultation case-mix.[<u>\[55\]</u>](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20660499)  
  
- Sports data come mostly from collegiate athletes and older bone-scan series, which under-represent degenerative forefoot conditions and probably under-count navicular and calcaneal injuries.[<u>\[56\]</u>](https://doi.org/10.1177/1071100715600495)  
  
- The full coded-diagnosis tables of the UK CiPCA and CPRD studies and the Taunton and Lievers diagnosis tables were not retrieved; they are the most likely sources of additional direct proportions.<u>[\[1\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20403912)[\[54\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31109927)[\[57\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11916889)[\[36\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31895592)</u>

**Where the evidence points against current values**

\- **P8:** The palpation data imply an LR+ of roughly 1.0–1.5 by calculation in acute-injury cohorts, well below the current 4. Specificity may be higher across all-cause ankle pain, but that is unmeasured.[<u>\[1\]</u>](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34286639)  
  
- **P9 and P10:** Because these items are the case definition, sensitivity is fixed by construction and an LR− of 0.3 has no empirical basis.<u>[\[2\]](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-017-0781-4)[\[3\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33789434)</u>  
  
- **P13:** A snapping or struck-from-behind sensation is also the classic description of tennis leg, and some ruptures are painless, so neither LR is supported.<u>[\[34\]](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.13812?utm_source=openevidence&utm_medium=referral)[\[35\]](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC13313361/)</u>  
  
- **P18:** In patients aged 50 or older, 73% of those with compartment syndrome still had symptoms after stopping exercise, which argues against "relieved by rest" as a rule-in feature.[<u>\[36\]</u>](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33278541)  
  
- **P27 and P28:** Acute calcium pyrophosphate arthritis and gout flares are frequently clinically indistinguishable, so redness or warmth cannot be assigned an LR for either.[<u>\[37\]</u>](https://doi.org/10.1002/art.40848?utm_source=openevidence&utm_medium=referral)  
  
Likelihood ratios marked "calculated" were derived arithmetically from published sensitivity and specificity or counts; the confidence intervals for CHEV-P26 were likewise calculated and are not the authors' own.

**Notes on specific rows**

\- **CHEV-P1:** The specificity of lateral swelling was not retrievable from the Baltes abstract, so no LR can be stated.[<u>\[1\]</u>](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38385771)  
  
- **CHEV-P2:** The published range caps LR+ at about 1.5 (sensitivity 100%, specificity 32%) and gives LR− of about 0.16 or lower. These two figures are arithmetic from the range endpoints, not published estimates.[<u>\[2\]</u>](https://doi.org/10.1177/19417381211029953)  
  
- **CHEV-P3:** Sensitivity is strongly time-dependent. In Baltes it was 21% (95% CI 7–46) at 0–2 days and 61% (95% CI 39–80) at 5–8 days, with acute specificity 100% (95% CI 77–100). Heterogeneity in the pooled estimate was high, probably from differing laxity thresholds between examiners.<u>[\[1\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38385771)[\[2\]](https://doi.org/10.1177/19417381211029953)</u>  
  
- **CHEV-P4, P5 and P7:** No retrieved study reports a pain-based or three-level result. Studies that counted subtle or intermediate laxity as negative did so within a binary result. The defensible evidence-based coding is two levels for the anterior drawer: laxity 3.97, no laxity 0.54.[<u>\[2\]</u>](https://doi.org/10.1177/19417381211029953)  
  
- **CHEV-P6 and P7:** The review found the talar tilt consistently specific and useful for ruling in CFL injury, but a negative test needs cautious interpretation.[<u>\[2\]</u>](https://doi.org/10.1177/19417381211029953)

**Limits of the evidence**

Only 3 of the 14 studies in the meta-analysis had low risk of bias across all domains. The classic delayed examination at 4–5 days (swelling, haematoma, ATFL tenderness and a positive drawer or talar tilt) has 96% sensitivity and 84% specificity, versus 71% and 33% acutely. These are composite figures and cannot be assigned to a single finding.<u>[\[2\]](https://doi.org/10.1177/19417381211029953)[\[5\]](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2025/1200/acute-ankle-sprains)</u> The per-sign data from van Dijk 1996 were not retrievable and should be checked in the original papers.

**Points that bear on the current estimates**

\- **Trauma-based figures do not transfer.** In UK general practice, 79% of foot/ankle consultations were non-traumatic and "ankle pain" was only 10% of foot/ankle codes. Sprain and fracture shares from acute-injury cohorts will overstate their share of all ankle-pain consultations.[<u>\[1\]</u>](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20403912)  
  
- **The under-18 sprain figure is the closest match.** The current 30–38% sits near the UK primary care range of 24–39%, but that denominator is foot plus ankle codes in 8–18-year-olds.[<u>\[3\]</u>](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-025-06296-y)  
  
- **CHEV-F3 sex multiplier** is larger than the limited general practice data support.<u>[\[10\]](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-019-2629-6)[\[11\]](https://doi.org/10.1155/2017/7021862?utm_source=openevidence&utm_medium=referral)</u>  
  
- **CHEV-F11** peaks before the stated population starts: mean age at diagnosis is 10.8 years, so the 9- and 11-year rows fall outside a ≥12 cohort.[<u>\[43\]</u>](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02328-w)  
  
- **CHEV-F16 and F18 age shape** may be too young: peroneal abnormalities and military talar lesion incidence both skew older.<u>[\[58\]](https://doi.org/10.1177/1071100716635645)[\[62\]](https://doi.org/10.3113/fai.2011.0948)</u>  
  
- **CHEV-F6 and F19** carry no sex multiplier, although published data show a female excess in older adults for fracture and after sprain for tarsal tunnel syndrome.<u>[\[24\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41616724)[\[45\]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35944572)</u>

**Caveats on sourcing**

PMIDs are given only where retrieved; the rest need checking before citation. Three figures are secondary citations whose primary papers were not retrieved: the 3.3% posterior tibial tendon prevalence, the 15% ankle involvement in gout, and the high ankle sprain share from Hopkinson.<u>[\[46\]](https://doi.org/10.1177/1071100714551020)[\[67\]](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/acr.20670?utm_source=openevidence&utm_medium=referral)[\[52\]](https://doi.org/10.1177/107110079001000607)</u> Settling the table would require a coded primary care database analysis of first ankle-pain consultations linked to final diagnosis by age and sex.  
  
Would it help to derive modelled proportions by dividing each condition's published primary care incidence by the age-specific ankle consultation rate, clearly labelled as estimates rather than published figures?
